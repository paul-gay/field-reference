## Highlight warnings ("blinkies")
In playback (and sometimes live), areas with no detail flash or show a color. They tell you **where** you've lost detail, which the histogram can't.
- Blinking sky or a white shirt: reduce exposure.
- Blinking light bulbs, the sun or reflections on chrome: usually fine. Those are meant to be white.

## Zebras
Diagonal stripes over areas above a brightness level you set. Mostly used for **video**, where there's no review image. Set them near the top of the range to catch clipping, or lower to check skin tones.

## Exposing to the right (ETTR)
On digital sensors, the brightest part of the range holds the most information and the least noise. ETTR means exposing as bright as you can **without clipping important highlights**, then darkening in editing.
- **Pros:** cleaner shadows, more editing room, especially at higher ISO.
- **Cons:** the preview looks too bright; you must edit every file; easy to overshoot.
- **Best for:** landscapes and still subjects shot in raw. **Skip it** for fast work, JPEG shooting, or when the camera's preview is what the client sees.

## Monochrom sensors
With no color filter, a Monochrom sensor gives clean shadows but very abrupt highlight clipping. Many Monochrom shooters do the opposite of ETTR: **protect the highlights first** and lift shadows in editing.

## Film is different
Color negative film handles overexposure gracefully and loses detail in underexposed shadows. With negative film, lean toward more light. Slide film is the opposite: protect the highlights.

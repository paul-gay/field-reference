## The idea
Ansel Adams and Fred Archer divided tones into **eleven zones**, from pure black (Zone 0) to pure white (Zone X). Each zone is **one stop** apart. Your meter always tries to put what it measures in **Zone V, middle gray**. The Zone System means deciding where a tone *should* go and adjusting exposure to put it there.

| Zone | Looks like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Pure black, no detail | Deep shadow at night |
| I to II | Near black, first hints of texture | Dark doorway |
| III | Dark with full texture | Dark fabric, shadowed bark |
| IV | Darker midtone | Dark foliage, shade on skin |
| **V** | **Middle gray: what the meter wants** | Gray card, weathered wood, clear north sky |
| VI | Lighter midtone | Typical light skin in sun |
| VII | Light with texture | Light skin highlights, pale concrete |
| VIII | Very light, faint texture | Textured snow, a white shirt |
| IX to X | Near white to pure white | Bright reflections, light sources |

## How to use it with any camera
1. **Spot meter the tone that matters most,** like a face or a snowfield.
2. **Decide which zone it belongs in.** Snow with texture: Zone VII or VIII. Light skin in sun: about Zone VI.
3. **Adjust exposure by the difference.** The meter puts snow in Zone V, so add two to three stops to place it in Zone VII or VIII.

## Why it's useful on the floor
It turns "the meter is wrong" into "the meter is doing exactly what it's designed to do." Great for Monochrom clients and film shooters, who think in tones rather than colors.
